## Problem

Design the backend that matches riders with drivers, like Uber or Ola. Asha opens the app at MG Road and asks for a ride. Within a few seconds, Ravi – a driver 600 m away – gets the request, accepts it, and Asha sees his car coming.

Millions of drivers send their GPS location every few seconds. The system must find nearby drivers fast and make sure one driver is never given two riders.

## Requirements

- Drivers send their location every 4 seconds while online.
- A rider requests a ride; we find nearby available drivers and offer the ride.
- A driver accepts or declines; the first acceptance wins.
- One driver can never be assigned to two trips at once.
- Match within a few seconds; works across many cities.

## Step 1: Clarify the requirements

> **💡 Tip**
> The two hard parts are a fast "who is near me?" query and a safe "assign this driver" step. Steer your questions toward them.

**Candidate:** How many drivers are online at once?

**Interviewer:** About 5 million worldwide at peak.

**Candidate:** How often do they send location?

**Interviewer:** Every 4 seconds.

**Candidate:** Do we offer a ride to one driver at a time or to several?

**Interviewer:** Your choice – explain the trade-off.

**Candidate:** Are pricing, payments and ETA prediction in scope?

**Interviewer:** No. Location, matching and assignment.

**Candidate:** How fresh must locations be?

**Interviewer:** A few seconds old is fine.

**Functional requirements**

1. Ingest driver locations.
2. Find nearby available drivers for a ride request.
3. Offer, accept and assign a driver; track the trip state.

**Non-functional requirements**

1. Match in under a few seconds.
2. Never double-assign a driver; never lose a confirmed trip.
3. 5 million drivers, scaling city by city.

## Step 2: Estimate the scale

> **💡 Tip**
> Location updates dominate the traffic, and they are throwaway data. That tells you where to keep them.

**Location updates**

```text
Online drivers         = 5 million
Updates per second     = 5M / 4 s          = 1.25 million per second
Size per update        ≈ 100 bytes
Ingest                 ≈ 125 MB/s
```

What it means: 1.25 million writes a second is too many for a normal database, but we only need each driver's **latest** location – an in-memory store fits.

**Ride requests**

```text
Trips per day          = 20 million
Average                = 20M / 86,400 s    ≈ 230 per second (peak ~1,000)
```

What it means: matching is much lower volume; it can afford careful, transactional work.

## Step 3: Define the API

```http
POST /v1/drivers/me/location    { "lat": 12.9756, "lng": 77.6050, "heading": 90 }   → 204
PUT  /v1/drivers/me/status      { "status": "AVAILABLE" }                           → 204

POST /v1/rides                  { "pickup": {...}, "drop": {...} }                   → 201 { "rideId": "...", "status": "SEARCHING" }
POST /v1/offers/{offerId}/accept                                                    → 200 | 409 (already taken)
GET  /v1/rides/{rideId}                                                             → status, driver, location
```

Drivers usually send location over a persistent connection rather than one HTTP request each time; the payload is the same.

> **🎯 In the interview**
> Point out that `accept` can return 409. Two drivers may tap Accept on the same ride; only one can win.

## Step 4: Define the data model

| Store | Contents | Technology |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Driver locations | driverId → (lat, lng, cell, updatedAt); cell → drivers | Redis (geo commands or our own cell sets), in memory |
| Driver status | driverId → AVAILABLE / OFFERED / ON_TRIP, currentTripId | Database row (or Redis) updated atomically |
| Trips | rideId, riderId, driverId, status, pickup, drop, times | SQL, sharded by city |
| Location history | trip route points | Append-only log, later cold storage |

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> Keep live locations only in memory. If Redis restarts, drivers resend within 4 seconds and the index rebuilds itself. Trips, which are money and promises, go in a durable database.

## Step 5: Draw the high-level design

[![High-level design: driver apps send GPS every 4 seconds to the location service, which updates a geo index in Redis; a rider's request goes to the matching service, which asks the geo index for nearby drivers, sends a ride offer, and assigns exactly one driver in the trip store.](designs/hld-9.svg)](designs/hld-9.svg)

**Location path**

1. Ravi's app sends his location every 4 seconds.
2. The location service converts it to a map cell and updates Redis: Ravi's latest position and which cell he is in.

**Matching path**

1. Asha requests a ride. The matching service finds her cell and the neighbouring cells.
2. It reads available drivers there and ranks them by estimated pickup time.
3. It offers the ride to the best driver (Ravi) with a 10-second timeout.
4. Ravi accepts. The service atomically sets Ravi from OFFERED to ON_TRIP for this ride and creates the trip.
5. Asha's app shows Ravi and his live location.

![Animation: Asha requests a ride; the matching service looks up her cell and its neighbours, finds Ravi 600 metres away and Chetan 1.2 kilometres away, offers the ride to Ravi, Ravi accepts, and the service atomically marks Ravi on trip and creates the trip.](learning-figures/hld-9-match.gif)

## Step 6: Go deep

### Finding nearby drivers: the geo index

**Option 1: Scan all drivers and compute distances.**

- Good: trivial.
- Problem: 5 million distance calculations per request – far too slow.

**Option 2: A database with latitude/longitude indexes.**

- Good: familiar.
- Problem: two separate range indexes don't combine well, and the index churns with 1.25 million updates a second.

**Option 3: Divide the map into cells (geohash, S2 or Uber's H3).**

- Good: a driver belongs to one cell; "nearby" means "this cell plus its neighbours" – a few small set lookups.
- Problem: cell size must be tuned: too large returns too many drivers, too small needs many neighbours.

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> Hexagonal cells (H3) of roughly 0.5–1 km across, stored in Redis as `cell → set of available drivers`. When Ravi moves into a new cell, remove him from the old set and add him to the new one.

[![Geo cells: the city is divided into cells; Asha's request looks in her own cell and the ring of neighbouring cells, collects available drivers, and ranks them by estimated pickup time instead of straight-line distance.](learning-figures/hld-9-cells.svg)](learning-figures/hld-9-cells.svg)

### Assigning safely: one driver, one trip

Two problems can cause double assignment: two rides offered to Ravi at once, or two drivers accepting the same ride.

The fix is one atomic step on both records:

```sql
UPDATE drivers SET status = 'ON_TRIP', trip_id = :rideId
 WHERE driver_id = :ravi AND status = 'OFFERED' AND offer_ride_id = :rideId;
-- 1 row updated → Ravi is ours; 0 rows → offer expired or Ravi took another ride

UPDATE rides SET status = 'ASSIGNED', driver_id = :ravi
 WHERE ride_id = :rideId AND status = 'SEARCHING';
-- both in one transaction (rides and drivers sharded by the same city)
```

> **⚠️ Common mistake**
> Assigning straight from the geo index. The index is a fast, slightly stale *hint* of who is nearby. The driver's status row is the authority on who is free.

### Offer one at a time, or broadcast?

**One at a time:** fair and simple, but every decline adds 10 seconds. **Broadcast to three:** faster, but two drivers will often tap Accept and one gets a disappointing 409. Most systems offer to one or a small batch, ranked by pickup time.

### Scaling by city

Matching for Bengaluru never needs drivers in Mumbai. Shard the location index, the matching service and the trip database by city or region. Each city's load is independent and a problem in one city stays there.

## Step 7: Find bottlenecks and failure points

| What fails | What happens | How we handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Redis node restarts | Location index empty | Drivers resend in 4 s; the index refills itself |
| Driver's app loses network | Stale position, ride offered to a ghost | Ignore drivers not seen for 15 s; offers time out after 10 s |
| Two drivers accept at once | Double assignment | Conditional update: only one succeeds, the other gets 409 |
| New Year's Eve in one city | Requests spike | City-level sharding; surge pricing reduces demand |
| Matching service crashes mid-offer | Ride stuck in SEARCHING | Offers have deadlines; a sweeper re-queues the ride |

## Putting it together

- **Location:** GPS every 4 s → cell → Redis sets, latest value only.
- **Match:** own cell + neighbours → rank by pickup time → offer with timeout.
- **Assign:** one conditional transaction marks the driver and the trip; sharded by city.

## Interviewer follow-ups

**Why not store location history in the same place?** History is append-only and needed later (billing, disputes); live location needs only the latest value. Different stores.

**How do you compute pickup time?** Road distance and traffic from a routing service, not straight-line distance – a driver across a river may be 300 m away and 15 minutes by road.

**How do you handle surge?** Count open requests and available drivers per cell every minute; raise the price where demand exceeds supply.

**What about pooled rides?** Matching becomes an optimisation over several riders' routes; the same atomic assignment rule applies to each seat.

## Wrap up

We kept latest driver locations in an in-memory cell index for fast lookups, ranked nearby drivers, and assigned exactly one with a conditional transaction.

> **📝 Key takeaways**
> - Location updates are huge and disposable: keep only the latest, in memory.
> - Cells (geohash, S2, H3) turn "nearby" into a few set lookups.
> - The geo index is a hint; the driver's status row decides.
> - Shard everything by city.

## Going deeper (optional)

The detailed version handles a driver moving between shards mid-offer and a slow old offer arriving after a newer one, using offer IDs and versions.

## Closing answer

"Drivers send GPS every 4 seconds – about 1.25 million updates a second – so I keep only each driver's latest position in Redis, indexed by map cell using H3 or geohash. When Asha requests a ride, the matching service looks in her cell and its neighbours, ranks available drivers by estimated pickup time and offers the ride to the best one with a timeout. Acceptance is one conditional transaction: the driver moves from OFFERED to ON_TRIP only if still offered this ride, and the ride from SEARCHING to ASSIGNED. The geo index is a fast hint; the status row is the authority. Everything is sharded by city."

## Problem

Design Google Drive or Dropbox. Asha saves a 200 MB presentation on her laptop. A minute later it appears on her phone, and her colleague Bilal, who has edit access, sees the new version too.

When Asha changes one slide, the app should not upload all 200 MB again. And if Asha and Bilal edit the same file offline, neither person's work may be silently lost.

## Requirements

- Upload, download and delete files and folders.
- Sync changes automatically to all of a user's devices.
- Share files and folders with view or edit permission.
- Keep previous versions.
- Upload only the changed parts of a large file; never lose an edit on conflict.

## Step 1: Clarify the requirements

> **💡 Tip**
> Separate the file **bytes** from the file **metadata** (names, folders, versions). Almost every design decision follows from that split.

**Candidate:** How large can files be?

**Interviewer:** Up to 50 GB.

**Candidate:** How many users?

**Interviewer:** 500 million users, 100 million daily active.

**Candidate:** Do we need real-time co-editing like Google Docs?

**Interviewer:** No – whole-file sync like Dropbox.

**Candidate:** What happens when two people change the same file?

**Interviewer:** Don't lose either change.

**Candidate:** Should it work offline?

**Interviewer:** Yes. Sync when the device reconnects.

**Functional requirements**

1. Upload, download, delete, move files and folders.
2. Sync across devices; share with view/edit permissions.
3. Version history; conflict copies when edits clash.

**Non-functional requirements**

1. Durability: never lose a file (11 nines, like S3).
2. Sync changes within seconds to a minute.
3. Save bandwidth: upload only changed chunks.

## Step 2: Estimate the scale

> **💡 Tip**
> Estimate storage and the number of sync notifications; both are much larger than you might expect.

**Storage**

```text
Users                  = 500 million
Average stored         ≈ 10 GB per user
Total                  = 500M × 10 GB     = 5 EB (before de-duplication)
```

What it means: bytes go in object storage, split into chunks, and identical chunks are stored once.

**Changes**

```text
Daily active users     = 100 million
File changes per user  ≈ 20 per day
Changes                = 2 billion per day ≈ 23,000 per second
```

What it means: the metadata database takes tens of thousands of writes a second and must be sharded, and devices must be told about changes efficiently.

## Step 3: Define the API

```http
POST /v1/files/{fileId}/versions/start   { "chunks": ["sha256:ab12…", "sha256:9f3c…", …] }
→ 200 { "missing": ["sha256:9f3c…"], "uploadUrls": { "sha256:9f3c…": "https://…" } }

PUT  {uploadUrl}                          (raw chunk bytes, straight to object storage)

POST /v1/files/{fileId}/versions/commit  { "baseVersion": 6, "chunks": [ … ] }
→ 201 { "version": 7 }   |   409 { "error": "CONFLICT", "currentVersion": 7 }

GET  /v1/changes?cursor=…                → list of changed files since cursor
POST /v1/files/{fileId}/share            { "email": "bilal@…", "role": "EDITOR" }
```

The client lists the chunk hashes it has; the server says which ones it is missing. Only those are uploaded.

> **🎯 In the interview**
> `baseVersion` on commit is the key detail. It says "I edited version 6". If the server is already at version 7, that is a conflict, not an overwrite.

## Step 4: Define the data model

| Table | Key fields |
| --- | --- |
| `file` | fileId, ownerId, parentFolderId, name, latestVersion, deleted |
| `file_version` | fileId, version, chunk list (ordered hashes), size, createdBy, createdAt |
| `chunk` | hash → storage location, reference count |
| `share` | fileId or folderId, userId, role (VIEWER / EDITOR) |
| `change_log` | userId or namespace, sequence, fileId, change type |

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> Metadata in a sharded SQL database (by owner or namespace), because moves, renames and permissions need transactions. Chunks in object storage, addressed by their content hash.

## Step 5: Draw the high-level design

[![High-level design: the sync client talks to the metadata service, which records version manifests and checks access policy; chunk bytes go to the chunk object store; every committed change is appended to a change log that other devices read; a garbage collector removes chunks no version uses.](designs/hld-10.svg)](designs/hld-10.svg)

**Asha saves the presentation**

1. Her sync client notices the change and splits the file into 4 MB chunks, hashing each one.
2. It asks the metadata service which hashes are new. Only slide 12 changed, so only one chunk is missing.
3. It uploads that one 4 MB chunk straight to object storage.
4. It commits version 7 = the ordered list of chunk hashes, with `baseVersion 6`.
5. The metadata service appends a change to the change log.

**Her phone and Bilal see it**

1. Their clients hold a long-poll (or push channel) on `/changes`.
2. They learn "file X is now version 7", fetch the manifest, download only chunks they don't already have, and rebuild the file.

![Animation: Asha edits slide 12; the client splits the file into chunks and only one chunk hash is new; it uploads that one chunk, commits version 7, the change log records it, and her phone downloads just the changed chunk to rebuild the file.](learning-figures/hld-10-sync.gif)

## Step 6: Go deep

### Chunking and de-duplication

Splitting files into chunks gives three wins:

- **Delta sync:** change one slide, upload one chunk – not 200 MB.
- **Resumable transfers:** a failed upload resumes at the next chunk.
- **De-duplication:** the same chunk (a shared PDF, a common library) is stored once, however many users have it.

Fixed 4 MB chunks are simple. Inserting a byte at the start shifts every boundary, though, so some systems use **content-defined chunking**, where boundaries follow the content and survive insertions.

### Conflicts: two edits to the same version

**Option 1: Last writer wins.**

- Good: simple.
- Problem: Asha's or Bilal's work silently disappears.

**Option 2: Lock the file while someone edits.**

- Good: no conflicts.
- Problem: impossible offline, and a forgotten lock blocks everyone.

**Option 3: Detect with versions; keep both.**

- Good: nothing is lost; the user decides.
- Problem: users occasionally see a "conflicted copy".

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> Detect with `baseVersion`. Bilal commits version 7 first. Asha's commit says "based on 6", so the server refuses it with 409, and her client saves her edit as `Deck (Asha's conflicted copy).pptx`. This is what Dropbox does.

[![Conflict handling: Asha and Bilal both edit version 6 offline; Bilal commits first and becomes version 7; Asha's commit based on version 6 is rejected with a conflict, so her client saves her edit as a conflicted copy and both changes survive.](learning-figures/hld-10-conflict.svg)](learning-figures/hld-10-conflict.svg)

> **⚠️ Common mistake**
> Comparing modification timestamps to pick a winner. Device clocks are wrong, and "newer" does not mean "contains the other person's change".

### Telling devices about changes

Each user (or shared folder) has a change log with increasing sequence numbers. A device stores the last sequence it processed and asks "what changed after 8,451?". A long-poll or push notification wakes it when something new arrives, so it does not poll every few seconds.

### Sharing and permissions

Permissions are checked by the metadata service on every manifest read and every upload URL it hands out. Upload and download URLs are short-lived and signed, so a leaked link stops working quickly.

## Step 7: Find bottlenecks and failure points

| What fails | What happens | How we handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upload interrupted | Partial file | Chunks already uploaded are kept; the version is committed only when all exist |
| Two offline edits | Conflict | `baseVersion` check; conflicted copy |
| A very large shared folder | One change notifies thousands of devices | Change log per shared namespace; devices read it at their own pace |
| Chunk no longer used | Storage leak | Reference counts plus a garbage collector that deletes after a grace period |
| Metadata shard hot (one huge team) | Slow commits | Split by namespace; cache folder listings |

## Putting it together

- **Upload:** chunk and hash → upload only missing chunks → commit a version with `baseVersion`.
- **Sync:** change log + long-poll → fetch manifest → download missing chunks.
- **Safety:** conflicts become copies; chunks are de-duplicated and garbage-collected.

## Interviewer follow-ups

**Why not store the whole file in the database?** Databases are poor at huge blobs. Object storage is cheaper, more durable and serves bytes directly to clients.

**How do you restore an old version?** Commit a new version whose chunk list equals the old one – no bytes move.

**How do you handle deletes?** Mark the file deleted (trash) and keep its versions for 30 days; then drop references and let the garbage collector reclaim chunks.

**Is de-duplication across users a privacy risk?** It can reveal that someone else has a file. Many services de-duplicate only within an account, or encrypt per user.

## Wrap up

We split files into hashed chunks in object storage, kept versions and permissions in a metadata database, and synced devices through a change log with conflict detection.

> **📝 Key takeaways**
> - Bytes in object storage, metadata in a transactional database.
> - Content-hashed chunks give delta sync, resumable uploads and de-duplication.
> - `baseVersion` turns concurrent edits into a conflicted copy, never a silent overwrite.
> - Devices catch up from a change-log cursor.

## Going deeper (optional)

The detailed version verifies that every chunk in a manifest really exists before committing a version, so a half upload can never become the latest file.

[![A new file version references verified chunks, not a half upload.](lessons/hld-10-flow.svg)](lessons/hld-10-flow.svg)

Further reading: [Dropbox – Streaming file synchronization](https://dropbox.tech/infrastructure/streaming-file-synchronization) and [Amazon S3 data durability](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/DataDurability.html).

## Closing answer

"I separate bytes from metadata. The client splits a file into 4 MB chunks and hashes each; the server says which hashes it lacks, and only those are uploaded straight to object storage with signed URLs. A version is an ordered list of chunk hashes, committed in a sharded SQL metadata service with the version it was based on. If someone else committed first, the commit is rejected and the client saves a conflicted copy, so no edit is lost. Every commit appends to a change log; other devices long-poll it, fetch the manifest and download only missing chunks. Chunks are de-duplicated and garbage-collected by reference count."

## Problem

Design web search like Google, at a high level. Asha types **"spring boot rate limiter"** and, in about 200 ms, gets ten relevant pages with titles and short snippets – chosen from billions of pages on the web.

Search has two halves: an offline half that crawls the web and builds an index, and an online half that answers queries from that index very fast.

## Requirements

- Crawl billions of web pages and keep them reasonably fresh.
- Build an index that maps words to the pages containing them.
- Answer keyword queries with the top 10 results, titles and snippets, in about 200 ms.
- Rank results by relevance and page quality.
- Handle about 100,000 queries per second.

## Step 1: Clarify the requirements

> **💡 Tip**
> Say up front that you will split the design into crawl → index → serve. Interviewers want to see that separation.

**Candidate:** How many pages should we index?

**Interviewer:** About 10 billion.

**Candidate:** How fresh must results be?

**Interviewer:** News within minutes would be nice; most pages within days or weeks is fine.

**Candidate:** Query volume?

**Interviewer:** About 100,000 queries a second at peak.

**Candidate:** Do we need images, maps, ads, spelling correction?

**Interviewer:** No. Plain web results.

**Candidate:** How sophisticated should ranking be?

**Interviewer:** Explain the main signals; no machine-learning deep dive.

**Functional requirements**

1. Crawl pages politely and repeatedly.
2. Build an inverted index.
3. Serve ranked top-10 results with snippets.

**Non-functional requirements**

1. Query latency about 200 ms.
2. Always available; a slightly stale index is acceptable.
3. Index billions of pages across thousands of machines.

## Step 2: Estimate the scale

> **💡 Tip**
> Size the crawl and the index separately. They are different problems.

**Crawling**

```text
Pages                 = 10 billion
Refresh every ~10 days → 1 billion pages per day ≈ 11,600 pages per second
Average page          ≈ 100 KB HTML → about 1.2 GB/s of downloads
```

What it means: crawling is a large distributed job with thousands of fetchers.

**Index**

```text
Text per page after cleaning   ≈ 10 KB
Raw text                       = 10B × 10 KB  = 100 TB
Inverted index (compressed)    ≈ 30–50% of text ≈ 30–50 TB
```

What it means: the index does not fit on one machine; it must be split into shards, and each shard copied for query throughput.

## Step 3: Define the API

```http
GET /v1/search?q=spring+boot+rate+limiter&page=1
→ 200 {
  "results": [
    { "url": "https://…", "title": "Rate limiting in Spring Boot…", "snippet": "…use a token bucket…" }
  ],
  "estimatedTotal": 1840000
}
```

> **🎯 In the interview**
> The public API is tiny. The interesting design is behind it: how the index is built and how one query fans out to many machines.

## Step 4: Define the data model

| Store | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| URL frontier | URLs to fetch, priority, next fetch time, per-host queues |
| Document store | url, fetched HTML/text, fetch time, content hash |
| Inverted index (per shard) | term → posting list of (docId, positions, term frequency) |
| Document metadata | docId → url, title, PageRank score, language |
| Index generations | which index version each shard is currently serving |

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> Split the index **by document** (each shard holds all terms for a subset of pages). A query goes to every shard, each returns its own top results, and a broker merges them. This keeps shards independent and balanced.

## Step 5: Draw the high-level design

[![High-level design: the crawler frontier feeds fetch-and-parse workers that store documents; index builders turn documents into index generations; the query broker sends each query to all index shards and the rank-and-snippets stage returns the top results.](designs/hld-11.svg)](designs/hld-11.svg)

**Offline: crawl and index**

1. The **frontier** holds URLs to fetch, ordered by priority and grouped by host.
2. **Fetchers** download pages, respecting `robots.txt` and a polite rate per site.
3. **Parsers** extract text, title and links; new links go back into the frontier.
4. **Index builders** turn documents into posting lists and publish a new **index generation** to the shards.

**Online: answer a query**

1. Asha's query reaches a **broker**, which normalises it to terms: `spring`, `boot`, `rate`, `limiter`.
2. The broker sends the query to every index shard (one replica of each).
3. Each shard intersects the posting lists, scores matches and returns its top 10.
4. The broker merges the shards' lists, picks the overall top 10, and fetches snippets.

![Animation: a query is split into four terms; the broker fans it out to every index shard; each shard intersects posting lists and returns its own top ten; the broker merges them into one global top ten and adds snippets.](learning-figures/hld-11-query.gif)

## Step 6: Go deep

### The inverted index

A book's index maps a word to page numbers. A search index does the same: each term points to a sorted list of the documents that contain it.

```text
spring   → [3, 17, 42, 108, 977, …]
boot     → [17, 42, 300, 977, …]
limiter  → [42, 977, 5120, …]
```

For "spring boot limiter", walk the sorted lists together and keep documents present in all of them: 42 and 977. Because the lists are sorted, this intersection is fast and needs no random access.

[![Inverted index: documents are split into terms; each term maps to a sorted posting list of document IDs; a query intersects the posting lists of its terms and only the shared documents are scored.](learning-figures/hld-11-index.svg)](learning-figures/hld-11-index.svg)

### Ranking: the scoring algorithm

Each candidate document gets a score from several signals:

- **Text relevance (BM25):** the query words appear often in this page and are rare across the web; title matches count more.
- **Link authority (PageRank):** many good pages link to it.
- **Freshness, language, location** and – at Google's scale – machine-learned models over hundreds of signals.

### Crawling politely and efficiently

**Option 1: A single global queue of URLs.**

- Good: simple.
- Problem: fetchers hammer one popular site, and important pages wait behind junk.

**Option 2: A frontier with per-host queues and priorities.**

- Good: one connection per host at a polite rate; important or fast-changing pages are fetched more often.
- Problem: more bookkeeping.

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> Per-host queues with priorities. News front pages are revisited every few minutes; a rarely changing page every few weeks. Content hashes detect duplicate pages so they are indexed once.

> **⚠️ Common mistake**
> Updating the live index in place while queries read it. A query could see half of a shard's new data. Build a new index generation, then switch shards to it atomically.

### Serving fast

Every shard has several replicas, so 100,000 queries a second are spread across copies. Popular queries ("weather", "cricket score") are cached at the broker. If one shard is slow, the broker can return results from the others rather than wait – slightly less complete, but on time.

## Step 7: Find bottlenecks and failure points

| What fails | What happens | How we handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A site blocks or slows our crawler | Fetches fail | Respect robots.txt and back off per host |
| Crawler traps (infinite calendars) | Endless URLs | Limit depth and URLs per host; detect patterns |
| One index shard is slow | Every query waits for it | Replicas, hedged requests, and a time budget per shard |
| Index build has a bug | Bad results everywhere | Validate a generation before switching; keep the previous one to roll back |
| Popular query spike | Hot load | Result cache at the broker |

## Putting it together

- **Crawl:** prioritised, polite frontier → fetch → parse → new links.
- **Index:** documents → posting lists → a new generation, switched atomically.
- **Serve:** broker fans out to all shards → each scores its top 10 → merge → snippets.

## Interviewer follow-ups

**Why shard by document rather than by term?** With term sharding, a popular term's shard becomes a hotspot and multi-word queries must gather huge lists across machines. Document sharding keeps every shard's work similar.

**How do you get news into results within minutes?** A small, frequently rebuilt "fresh" index alongside the big one; the broker queries both.

**How do you handle typos?** A spelling-correction step before search, using query logs and edit distance.

**How do you make the result page fast?** Cache popular queries, pre-compute snippets where possible, and stop waiting for slow shards after the time budget.

## Wrap up

We crawled politely into a document store, built an inverted index in versioned generations, and answered queries by fanning out to all shards and merging their top results.

> **📝 Key takeaways**
> - Offline crawl and index; online serve. Keep them separate.
> - An inverted index maps each term to a sorted list of documents.
> - Shard by document; the broker merges each shard's top results.
> - Rank with text relevance plus link authority and other signals.

## Going deeper (optional)

[![Query serving reads a published index generation rather than crawling on demand.](lessons/hld-11-flow.svg)](lessons/hld-11-flow.svg)

## Closing answer

"I split search into crawl, index and serve. A frontier with per-host queues and priorities feeds polite fetchers that respect robots.txt; parsers extract text and links, and new links go back into the frontier. Index builders turn documents into an inverted index – each term maps to a sorted posting list – sharded by document and published as versioned generations. At query time a broker sends the terms to every shard; each intersects posting lists, scores candidates with BM25, PageRank and other signals, and returns its top ten. The broker merges them, adds snippets and caches popular queries. Replicas and time budgets keep latency near 200 ms."

5. Fulfilment picks it up: pack, ship, deliver.

## Step 6: Go deep

### Checkout across services: the saga

Order, inventory and payment are separate services with separate databases, so one database transaction cannot cover them.

**Option 1: A distributed transaction (two-phase commit).**

- Good: all-or-nothing.
- Problem: slow, fragile, and payment gateways do not take part in your 2PC.

**Option 2: A saga – a sequence of local steps, each with an undo.**

- Good: each step is a simple local transaction; failures run compensations.
- Problem: there are visible in-between states (`PENDING_PAYMENT`) to handle.

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> An **orchestrated saga** run by the order service: reserve stock → take payment → confirm. If payment fails or times out, compensate by releasing the reservation and cancelling the order.

[![Checkout saga: create a pending order, reserve inventory, take payment; on success confirm the reservation and the order; on payment failure release the reservation and cancel; on timeout check the payment status before deciding.](learning-figures/hld-12-saga.svg)](learning-figures/hld-12-saga.svg)

### Not overselling

Reserving is one atomic, conditional update:

```sql
UPDATE inventory
   SET available = available - 1, reserved = reserved + 1
 WHERE product_id = :shoe AND warehouse = 'BLR-1' AND available >= 1;
-- 1 row → reserved; 0 rows → out of stock, tell Asha before she pays
```

Reservations expire after 10 minutes, so an abandoned checkout returns the stock.

> **⚠️ Common mistake**
> Checking stock in the product page cache and treating it as truth. The badge can be seconds old. Only the inventory service's conditional update decides.

### Sale-day spikes

- Serve product pages almost entirely from CDN and caches.
- Put a **waiting room** (queue) in front of checkout for the hottest deals, letting in a steady flow.
- Split a hot item's stock into several inventory rows ("buckets") so many reservations don't fight over one row.

### Reliable events between services

When the order becomes `CONFIRMED`, fulfilment and email must hear about it exactly when it commits. Write the status change and an "order confirmed" event in the same transaction (an **outbox** table); a relay publishes the event to Kafka.

## Step 7: Find bottlenecks and failure points

| What fails | What happens | How we handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payment times out | We don't know if Asha paid | Keep the reservation, ask the gateway for status, decide then |
| Asha retries checkout | Two orders | Idempotency key returns the first order |
| Search index lags | New price shows late | Product page reads price from the price service; search is a hint |
| Last item, many buyers | Contention on one row | Conditional update; bucketed stock; waiting room |
| Fulfilment service down | Confirmed orders wait | Events stay in Kafka until it recovers |

## Putting it together

- **Browse:** CDN + caches + a denormalised search index.
- **Checkout:** saga – reserve with a conditional update, pay, confirm; compensate on failure.
- **Scale:** separate services and databases; outbox events; waiting room on sale days.

## Interviewer follow-ups

**Why reserve before payment instead of after?** So Asha is never charged for shoes that sold out. A reservation with a timeout holds stock briefly without blocking it forever.

**How does search stay in sync with the catalogue?** Catalogue changes publish events; an indexer updates Elasticsearch within seconds.

**How do you handle multiple sellers in one order?** Split the order into one shipment per seller or warehouse; each has its own fulfilment status.

**What if payment succeeds after the reservation expired?** The confirm step finds the reservation gone; try to reserve again, and if stock is gone, refund automatically.

## Wrap up

We served browsing from caches and a search index, and ran checkout as a saga with an atomic stock reservation, payment and compensation.

> **📝 Key takeaways**
> - Reads (browse) and writes (checkout) get different designs.
> - Each service owns its data; a saga with compensations replaces a distributed transaction.
> - A conditional update reserves stock; reservations expire.
> - Idempotency keys and an outbox make retries and events safe.

- Browse movies, cinemas and shows by city and date.
- View the seat map for a show and hold chosen seats for a few minutes.

## Step 1: Clarify the requirements

> **💡 Tip**

**Candidate:** How many cities, cinemas and shows?

**Interviewer:** 500 cities, 10,000 screens, about 50,000 shows a day.

**Non-functional requirements**

## Step 2: Estimate the scale

## Step 6: Go deep

### Holding seats without double-booking

**Option 1: Check, then update in two steps.**

- Good: easy to write.
- Problem: Asha and Bilal both see F8 free and both update – a classic race.

**Option 2: Pessimistic locks (`SELECT … FOR UPDATE`).**

- Good: correct.
- Problem: locks pile up under a spike and slow everyone.

**Option 3: One conditional update per hold.**

- Good: correct and short; the database does the check and the write together.
- Problem: callers must handle "0 rows changed".

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> A single conditional update for all requested seats:

```sql
UPDATE show_seat
   SET status = 'HELD', hold_id = :hold, hold_expires_at = :now + interval '8 minutes'
 WHERE show_id = :show AND seat_no IN ('F7','F8')
   AND (status = 'AVAILABLE' OR (status = 'HELD' AND hold_expires_at < :now));
-- 2 rows → hold succeeded; fewer → roll back and report which seats are taken
```

Expired holds are treated as free immediately, even before the cleanup job runs.

### The waiting room

Letting 3,300 people a second hit the booking service would overload the database and fail everyone. A **virtual queue** gives each arriving user a position and admits, say, 500 users a second. Users see "You are number 41,230 – about 6 minutes". The system stays fast for those inside.

[![Opening-day traffic: two million arrivals enter a virtual waiting room, which admits a steady 500 users per second; admitted users read cached seat maps and make atomic holds; unpaid holds expire and seats return.](learning-figures/hld-13-waiting-room.svg)](learning-figures/hld-13-waiting-room.svg)

### Payment and expiry together

Payment may succeed just after the hold expired and Bilal grabbed the seat. The confirm step must therefore also be conditional: `status = 'HELD' AND hold_id = :asha`. If that fails, Asha's payment is refunded automatically.

> **⚠️ Common mistake**
> Using a Redis key with a TTL as the only record of a hold, while bookings live in SQL. Two stores can disagree – the key expires, the seat is resold, and Asha's late payment still books it. Keep one authority for seat state.

| What fails | What happens | How we handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 million users arrive at once | Database overload | Waiting room admits a steady rate |
| Asha closes the app | Seats stuck as HELD | Holds expire after 8 minutes; treated as free once expired |
| Payment succeeds after expiry | Seat may be resold | Conditional confirm; refund if lost |
| Seat-map cache is stale | Asha picks a taken seat | The hold fails with 409 and the map refreshes |
| Counter sales and app sales | Two channels race | Both use the same conditional update |

**What about seat-selection fairness for bots?** CAPTCHA and per-account limits at the waiting-room entrance.

**How do you release expired holds?** A periodic job flips them back to AVAILABLE for the map; correctness already treats them as free.

We cached the read path, smoothed spikes with a waiting room, and held seats with one conditional update that expires automatically.

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> SQL with strong transactions. Money needs constraints (unique idempotency keys, balanced ledger entries) more than it needs NoSQL scale.

## Step 5: Draw the high-level design

[![High-level design: the merchant API creates payment intents; a provider adapter calls the bank or card network; provider callbacks arrive in a webhook inbox; results are written to a double-entry ledger; a daily reconciliation job compares the ledger with provider settlement files.](designs/hld-14.svg)](designs/hld-14.svg)

**Paying ₹1,250**

1. The shop calls `POST /payments` with an idempotency key. We save the payment as `CREATED` (or return the existing one for a repeated key).
2. The router picks a provider (UPI switch) and records a `payment_attempt`, then calls it.
3. The provider answers – or later sends a callback – with success or failure.
4. In one transaction we set the payment to `SUCCEEDED` and write ledger entries.
5. We notify the shop by webhook (from an outbox, retried until acknowledged).
6. Each night we compare our records with the provider's settlement file.

## Step 6: Go deep

### Never charging twice: idempotency end to end

There are two places a duplicate can happen:

- **Shop → us:** the shop retries after a timeout. The unique `(merchantId, idempotencyKey)` returns the original payment.
- **Us → provider:** we retry a bank call. We send our stable `attemptId` as the provider's reference, so the provider recognises the repeat.

### The timeout: "unknown" is a real state

**Option 1: Treat a timeout as failure.**

- Good: simple.
- Problem: the bank may have charged Asha. Her shop shows "failed", she pays again – charged twice.

**Option 2: Retry immediately.**

- Good: might succeed.
- Problem: without a provider-side idempotency reference, the retry is a second charge.

**Option 3: Mark the attempt UNKNOWN and find out.**

- Good: no guess. Query the provider's status API, or wait for its callback.
- Problem: the shop waits a little longer for a final answer.

> **⚖️ Design choice**
> Option 3. A timeout moves the payment to `PENDING_CONFIRMATION`. A status check (and the provider's callback) decides SUCCEEDED or FAILED. Only a confirmed failure allows a fresh attempt.

[![Payment states: created, then processing with a provider attempt; success or failure is final; a timeout leads to an unknown state that is resolved by a status query or callback, never by guessing.](learning-figures/hld-14-states.svg)](learning-figures/hld-14-states.svg)

> **⚠️ Common mistake**
> Marking a payment FAILED because the HTTP call timed out. A timeout means "I don't know", and money systems must record not-knowing honestly.

### Double-entry ledger

Every money movement is written as balanced entries – total debits equal total credits:

```text
Payment ₹1,250 succeeded:
  debit   provider_receivable     125000
  credit  merchant_payable        122500
  credit  gateway_fee_revenue       2500
```

Entries are only appended, never updated. A refund adds new reversing entries. Balances are sums of entries, so they can always be audited.

### Webhooks in both directions

- **Incoming from providers:** store each event in an inbox with a unique provider event ID; processing twice is harmless.
- **Outgoing to merchants:** write the event in the same transaction as the status change (outbox); a sender retries with backoff until the shop replies 2xx. Sign the webhook so the shop can verify it came from us.

### Reconciliation

Every night, compare our ledger with each provider's settlement file. Mismatches – a payment the bank settled but we marked failed – become cases for automatic fixes or human review. This is the final safety net.

## Step 7: Find bottlenecks and failure points

| What fails | What happens | How we handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Provider times out | Unknown if charged | PENDING_CONFIRMATION; status query and callback decide |
| Shop retries the request | Possible double charge | Idempotency key returns the original payment |
| Callback arrives twice | Double processing | Inbox with unique provider event ID |
| One provider is down | Payments fail | Route to a healthy provider; circuit breaker |
| Our record and the bank's disagree | Money mismatch | Daily reconciliation and repair |

## Putting it together

- **Create:** idempotency key → save → route to a provider with a stable attempt reference.
- **Result:** success, failure or unknown; unknown is resolved by status checks and callbacks.
- **Money:** append-only double-entry ledger, outbox webhooks, nightly reconciliation.

## Interviewer follow-ups

**Where do card numbers live?** In a separate, locked-down vault service (PCI scope). Everything else uses a token that stands for the card.

**Authorise vs capture?** Authorise reserves the money on the card; capture takes it, for example when the order ships. Our state machine includes both.

**How do refunds work?** A new refund record, a provider call with its own idempotency, and reversing ledger entries – never editing the original payment.

**How do you pick a provider?** By method, success rate, cost and health; a router with circuit breakers moves traffic away from a failing one.

## Wrap up

We made every request idempotent, treated timeouts as unknown until confirmed, recorded money in an append-only double-entry ledger, and reconciled daily.

> **📝 Key takeaways**
> - Idempotency keys from the merchant, stable references to the provider.
> - A timeout is "unknown", never "failed".
> - Double-entry, append-only ledger; refunds are new entries.
> - Inbox/outbox for webhooks; nightly reconciliation catches the rest.
